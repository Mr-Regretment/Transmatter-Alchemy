Kern & The Cell

Ignition and Kern Redirection

Initiating Kern flow is not instantaneous. The clearest way to picture it: like pushing a boulder into a run, rather than flipping a switch. A practitioner has to ramp up to the output a technique requires, and that ramp-up takes real time — on the order of a minute for a full ignition — plus a flat Kern cost, during which the practitioner is at their most vulnerable, having committed effort without yet producing an effect. This window is precisely when a technique is most susceptible to disruption.

Once the boulder is moving, easing off doesn't stop it instantly — it slows. A practitioner can let flow taper down deliberately, but doing so means paying the ramp-up cost again to get it back to full speed, the same as stopping a boulder mid-push and having to shove it back into motion. There is no free pause; flow is either being maintained, decaying, or being rebuilt from a stop.

While flow is established, a practitioner can redirect the residual momentum from a completed reaction directly into the next reaction in a chain, bypassing the ignition cost for every subsequent link. This is the mechanical basis for chaining: a five-step sequence costs a single ignition plus five reaction costs, rather than five full ignitions. The mechanic rewards fluency, since maintaining a clean handoff between reactions requires genuine mastery of the underlying pattern.

If a chain is broken mid-sequence, the redirected flow disperses, and continuing requires paying the full ignition cost again — the boulder has to be pushed from a dead stop, not just nudged back up to speed.
